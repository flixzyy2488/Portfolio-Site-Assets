01 / About

Who I am

I'm an engineer at heart. I want to know exactly how something works, and then I want to design a better one.

That started early, with a habit of opening up anything technical to see what was inside. It grew into a need to understand the theory underneath: the physics, the math, and the design decisions that make a machine work. For the last three years I've been part of FRC Team 10121, the TMSA Mecha Tigers, where I've served as President and now Vice President with the role of organizing and mentoring all teams leads and all team members, and led the build, CAD, engineering and electrical teams. Leading there means organizing the team, teaching new members how to use the tools, and explaining why one design works better than another. We've taken multiple first- and second-place finishes at district events and reached the finals of the state championship. We did that as a new team, with little funding and without the mentors and school support that established teams rely on, and that gap is what kept us from winning the state title. 03 / Stack

What I work with

CAD / Design

Extensive CAD work in Fusion 360. I model full assemblies, check how every part fits and moves, and take a design from first sketch to something ready to produce.

Fabrication

Power tools, CNC machining, wiring and soldering. I machine precise parts on a CNC, cut, drill and assemble the rest by hand, build and solder the electrical system that runs it, then test it and fix what breaks.

Code / AI

I write the code that runs what I build, and use AI for research, design work and automation: autonomous control for UAVs and ground vehicles, and automated routines for FRC robots.

04 / On a team

The builder on your team

Plenty of people can spot a problem worth solving. Fewer can build the thing that solves it. That's where I come in.

The best teams pair people who see the problem with people who can build the answer. Say someone has an idea for low-cost prosthetic arms but has never designed a part in their life. I can take a concept like that and work out the mechanism, the materials and the most sufficient way to make it well.

Lead

I organize the team, teach people the tools, and walk everyone through the options so they know why we chose one.

Design

I turn a rough idea into a mechanism that works on paper before anyone spends money on it.

Build

Mechanical, electrical and hands-on fabrication. I prototype it, test it, and fix what breaks.

Cost

I look for the simplest design and the cheapest material that still does the job properly.

It started with the aerodynamics. MK23 Concrete Beam

A concrete beam designed to carry a fully loaded Oshkosh MK23, the Marine Corps' 7-ton truck. It placed first, deflecting only about a tenth of an inch under the load.

At the SAME engineering camp at Marine Corps Base Camp Lejeune, the challenge was to design a new concrete beam strong enough to hold a fully loaded MK23 cargo truck.

Every team worked to the same limits: a beam 48 inches long, a cross-section of exactly 24 square inches, and two pieces of rebar, 8 feet in total, to reinforce it. With the material fixed, the only way to win was to use it better.

I chose a 4 by 6 inch section and stood it on edge, so the 6 inches ran vertically. A beam's stiffness grows with the cube of its depth, so the same concrete set upright is 2.25 times stiffer than it would be lying flat. [Add: how the rebar was cut and placed, and why.]

I put the team together and led it from start to finish: organizing who did what, teaching teammates how to use the tools, and walking everyone through the design variations so they understood why some were stronger than others.

The design won first place. It held the load with roughly 1/10 inch of deflection, so the beam barely moved under the full weight of the truck. [Add: how it was tested and how the other designs compared.]
